## 1. Coverage, and an abstract anomaly worth its own paragraph

(incl. B.1), C (C.1, C.2), and D. Nothing below is limited by what I could read.

**The stored abstract does not match arXiv, and this one is the strangest of the
three.** `papers.sqlite` holds a longer abstract containing:

> "**Previous approaches based on random combinatorial search indicate a low
> success rate even when the circuit ansatz is nominally adequately
> parameterized, motivating the use of heavily overparameterized circuits.** In
> this work, we present a gradient-based optimization framework… **We prescribe
> parameter-optimal circuit skeletons and eliminate the need for random
> combinatorial search.**"

Neither the arXiv abs page nor the v1 HTML contains any of that. Both carry a
**shorter** abstract ending "…avoiding the random selection of certain
parameter-deficient circuit skeletons."

On the two previous cards the DB held text arXiv had revised away. Here the DB
holds text that appears in **no version I can reach**, and it is *more* detailed
than what arXiv shows — the opposite of the usual drift direction.

This matters directly: **the card's `problem` field is built on that sentence**
("Prior parameter-search approaches report low success… and respond by heavily
overparameterizing"). Judged against arXiv, that clause looks unsupported.
Judged against what the curator was shown, it is a faithful paraphrase. Three of
four cards now show this drift, and this is the cleanest demonstration that

## 2. Quote inventory

**Q1** — §1: "**Unitary synthesis** refers to the task of decomposing a unitary
operator, represented as a matrix, into a series of quantum gates natively
supported by hardware… this will generally produce circuits that are far from
optimal in size. Moreover, in general, **finding an optimal decomposition is
believed to be very hard** [5, 8]."

**Q2** — §1: "one approach is to posit a **circuit \"skeleton\" of parameterized
gates** and then search over the real parameter space to **minimize the error
between the target and synthesized unitary**. The resulting optimization problem
is **highly non-convex**, but earlier works [2, 3] have shown some forms of
success with **gradient descent-based approaches, sometimes referred to as
GRAPE** [6, 9]."

**Q3** — §1: "for a *generic* unitary on n-qubits, **a circuit of depth
$\Theta(4^n/n)$ is required, as given by parameter counting**."

**Q4** — §1: "Previous work [3] suggested that gradient descent could do this
sometimes, but **only with some probability that rapidly fell to zero as the
number of qubits grew**. In contrast, we find that – with one simple prescription
for selecting the circuit skeleton – gradient descent is able to find a
depth-optimal circuit every time. **These circuits naturally turn out to be
optimal in terms of the number of entangling gates, and in the number of total
gates, as well.**"

**Q5** — §1: "**Our investigations are empirical**, but strongly suggest that
gradient descent fails only when certain **bad circuit skeletons are selected,
resulting in an effectively underparameterized circuit**… gradient descent
continued to find optimal-size circuits even in **1D or star-topology qubit
layouts** and **never failed even across hundreds of trials**. As a result, **we
conjecture that the resulting loss landscape has no local minima**."

**Q6** — §2: "When the unitary is already given as a circuit, the goal is to find
a shorter or hardware-compatible circuit; **this process is typically referred to
as \"compiling\"**. Meanwhile, **\"synthesis\" typically refers to when the
unitary is given as the full $2^n\times 2^n$ matrix**."

**Q7** — §3.1: "We focus especially on the case where **the CNOT gate is the
unique two-qubit entangling gate, and arbitrary single-qubit unitaries are
allowed**… **The pattern of CNOTs dictates what we call the \"circuit
skeleton\"**, and as a discrete variable, it is held constant during gradient
descent… **our main innovation is noticing that, for generic unitaries, this
random search is unnecessary, and we can simply pick an ideal skeleton from the
get-go using a simple design principle.**"

**Q8** — §3.2: "we choose the **distance measure to be the trace distance**.
Therefore, $C = N - \mathrm{abs}(\mathrm{Tr}(D))$."

**Q9** — §3.2.1: "**we consider skipping several steps of the descent process by
singular value decomposition (SVD)**… we find the **closest unitary to $A_i$
through SVD** [14]."

**Q10** — §3.2.2: "the symmetries of the CNOT gates reduce the overall parameter
count… **These two symmetries effectively reduce 2 free parameters per CNOT**"
→ eq (7) $\ell = \lceil (4^n-1-n)/2n \rceil$. Also analyses **CZ, Sycamore, and
SWAP** parameter losses.

**Q11** — §4.1: "the same two qubits are paired together for four layers
consecutively… we end up using $3\cdot8-2\cdot3=18$ parameters to build what is
effectively a unitary that acts only on two qubits. However, a general two-qubit
unitary requires just 15 parameters. **The surplus parameters introduce
redundancies in the circuit, leading to symmetries**… **These symmetries reduce
the number of effective degrees of freedom available to the rest of the
circuit.**"

**Q12** — §4.1: "**This problem is a well-known graph theory problem, referred to
as the 1-factorization of a k-node graph.** The maximum number of combinations
corresponds to the number of 1-factors, which is given by $k-1$."

**Q13** — §4.2: "the optimization algorithm was run for around **200 two-qubit,
100 four-qubit and 30 six-qubit** unitaries… The target unitaries for all system
sizes were **generated Haar-randomly** using a function from the scipy library."

**Q14** — Appendix D: "For a given circuit topology, **we can compute its
effective parameters** using the principles outlined above. **Then if this
effective parameter count is less than $4^n-1$, failure is (generically)
necessary.** Any block of three CNOTs coupling the same pair of qubits in a row
creates an arbitrary two-qubit gate, giving only 15 parameters instead of the
$6+4\cdot3=18$… **This agrees very well with the numbers reported in [3].**"

**Q15** — Appendix D: "For larger n, the case work… becomes unwieldy to do on
paper, but **is easily calculated by a computer program**. We find excellent
agreement with the theory; for n=4, a <1% success chance with N=61 CNOT gates, a
50% success rate for N=64."

### `limitations` — **p**

Bullets 1, 2, 4 hold. Bullet 1 quotes Q5 correctly and correctly notes the
no-local-minima claim is a **conjecture**. Bullet 2 is accurate — Q13 confirms
only 2, 4, 6 qubits.

**Bullet 3 is overstated**: *"no general criterion is given for when a skeleton
is parameter-deficient beyond the specific prescription offered."*

Appendix D **does** give a criterion (Q14): compute the topology's effective
parameter count; if it is below $4^n-1$, failure is generically necessary. The
paper then applies it in closed form for n=3 and by program for n=4 and n=5
(Q15), matching the prior work's measured failure rates. It is not a
closed-form-for-all-n criterion, but "no general criterion is given" tells a
reader there is nothing, and there is a computable test.

The card's `open_questions` bullet 2 compounds it — *"Is there a general,
checkable criterion identifying parameter-deficient circuit skeletons a
priori?"* — posing as open a question Appendix D substantially answers.

**Same shape as 2608.17381's BoltzDesign bullet**: an absence asserted from
sections that could not contain the evidence. `sections_read` is `[abstract,
intro, conclusion]`; Appendix D is neither.

Bullet 5 is mildly overstated too — "only the arbitrary-single-qubit + CNOT gate
set is considered" is true of the *experiments*, but §3.2.2 (Q10) explicitly works
out parameter loss for **CZ, Sycamore, generic two-qubit, and SWAP**. Noted, not
weighted.

### Object 1: the paper explicitly denies the alias

`Unitary synthesis` carries `aliases: [quantum circuit synthesis, **unitary
compilation**, gate decomposition]`.

Q6 draws exactly this distinction and draws it deliberately:

> "When the unitary is already given as a circuit… this process is typically
> referred to as **\"compiling\"**. Meanwhile, **\"synthesis\"** typically refers
> to when the unitary is given as the full $2^n \times 2^n$ matrix."

Different input, different problem — and §2 devotes a paragraph to why compilers
(Qiskit, Quantum Shannon Decomposition) are *not* parameter-optimal, which is
half the paper's motivation. Listing "unitary compilation" as a synonym for
"unitary synthesis" merges two nodes the source separates on purpose.

`p`, consistent with 2606.07914's `mixing matrix` and 2608.17381's `trust region`
— one false-identity alias among otherwise correct ones. *(The marks I cleared
had this at `y`.)*

Object 6's name, `Circuit skeleton / ansatz topology`, is the **third**
slash-or-parenthesis compound name across five cards, after `Hermitization
(Girko's method)` and `Subset-rank and no-cancellation conditions`. Still the
cheapest validator rule available: reject a `name` containing `/`, `(`, or
` and `.
